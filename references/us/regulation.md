# Fact and regulation checklist (United States)

Applied to the CD's candidates in step 9, the client review (`09-client-review.md`). **Do not copy this into team prompts.** If you do, the constraints turn into the message of the copy.

## 0. Fact check (every run)

| Check | Question |
|---|---|
| Basis | Does every factual claim about the product (numbers, dates, conditions, prices, reviews, certifications, awards, results) have a basis in an F item in `02-raw.md` or in the reasons to believe / new findings in the creative brief (`03-creative-brief.md`)? |
| Source grade | If the basis is graded anything other than "provided by client", is it marked `[client to confirm]`? Where public sources disagree on a fact (X items), did the copy avoid picking one side silently? |
| Misreading | Reading the headline alone, could someone misunderstand a condition or limit? If so, record it as something the subhead or body must correct right away (but see 1.2: body copy may not cure a false headline). |
| Fictional label | Could a fictional scene or person read as a real review or case? |
| People's words | Has a quote from V (people's words) been used as if it were a review by a user of this product? Is there any information that identifies an individual? |

Sections 1 to 3 below are the regulatory checks.

> Warning: Every rule below is quoted from the primary source listed with it (checked 2026-10-02). Quotes are verbatim; `[...]` marks text left out, and footnote numbers in the FTC policy statements are dropped. This check only **flags** risky expressions. It is not legal advice and not a legal judgment.

## 1. General: FTC Act Section 5 and deception (check on every run)

### 1.1 FTC Act Section 5, 15 U.S.C. 45(a)(1)

> "Unfair methods of competition in or affecting commerce, and unfair or deceptive acts or practices in or affecting commerce, are hereby declared unlawful."

Source: https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/15/45 (checked 2026-10-02)

What to flag in a headline: any claim that could mislead, since every rule below hangs off this sentence.

### 1.2 FTC Policy Statement on Deception (October 14, 1983)

> "Certain elements undergird all deception cases. First, there must be a representation, omission or practice that is likely to mislead the consumer."

> "Thus, the Commission will find deception if there is a representation, omission or practice that is likely to mislead the consumer acting reasonably in the circumstances, to the consumer's detriment."

> "Depending on the circumstances, accurate information in the text may not remedy a false headline because reasonable consumers may glance only at the headline."

> "Pro forma statements or disclaimers may not cure otherwise deceptive messages or practices."

Source: https://www.ftc.gov/system/files/documents/public_statements/410531/831014deceptionstmt.pdf (landing page https://www.ftc.gov/legal-library/browse/ftc-policy-statement-deception) (checked 2026-10-02)

What to flag in a headline: a headline that is false or misleading on its own, even if a subhead, body or asterisk later corrects it; "free", "no fees", "instant" with conditions only in fine print.

### 1.3 FTC Policy Statement Regarding Advertising Substantiation (November 23, 1984)

> "The Commission intends to continue vigorous enforcement of this existing legal requirement that advertisers substantiate express and implied claims, however conveyed, that make objective assertions about the item or service advertised."

> "Therefore, a firm's failure to possess and rely upon a reasonable basis for objective claims constitutes an unfair and deceptive act or practice in violation of Section 5 of the Federal Trade Commission Act."

> "When the substantiation claim is express (e.g.., "tests prove", "doctors recommend", and "studies show"), the Commission expects the firm to have at least the advertised level of substantiation."

> "Of course, an ad may imply more substantiation than it expressly claims or may imply to consumers that the firm has a certain type of support; in such cases, the advertiser must possess the amount and type of substantiation the ad actually communicates to consumers."

Source: https://www.ftc.gov/legal-library/browse/ftc-policy-statement-regarding-advertising-substantiation (checked 2026-10-02; "e.g.." is as printed on the page)

What to flag in a headline: any objective claim (numbers, speed, "#1", "lasts 2x longer", "saves 3 hours") with no F item behind it, which in this skill should already carry `[client to confirm]`; establishment claims ("clinically proven", "tests show", "doctors recommend", "studies show", "scientifically designed") unless the raw material contains that level of proof (the study, the test, the doctors).

### 1.4 Comparative advertising, 16 CFR 14.15

> "Commission policy in the area of comparative advertising encourages the naming of, or reference to competitiors, but requires clarity, and, if necessary, disclosure to avoid deception of the consumer."

> "The Commission has supported the use of brand comparisions where the bases of comparision are clearly identified."

> "The Commission has previously held that disparaging advertising is permissible so long as it is truthful and not deceptive."

Source: https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-16/chapter-I/subchapter-B/part-14/section-14.15 (text fetched from the eCFR API, checked 2026-10-02; the misspellings "competitiors" and "comparisions" are in the official text)

What to flag in a headline: "better than [brand]", "2x faster than the leading app", "lowest price" when the comparison target, the attribute measured, or the basis is not stated and not in the raw material; negative claims about a named or identifiable competitor without a source.

## 2. General: endorsements, reviews and sponsorship (when the copy uses reviews, testimonials, influencers or affiliates)

### 2.1 Guides Concerning Use of Endorsements and Testimonials in Advertising, 16 CFR Part 255 (revised 88 FR 48102, July 26, 2023)

Material connection (16 CFR 255.5(a)):

> "When there exists a connection between the endorser and the seller of the advertised product that might materially affect the weight or credibility of the endorsement, and that connection is not reasonably expected by the audience, such connection must be disclosed clearly and conspicuously. Material connections can include a business, family, or personal relationship. They can include monetary payment or the provision of free or discounted products (including products unrelated to the endorsed product) to an endorser, regardless of whether the advertiser requires an endorsement in return."

What to flag in a headline: influencer, creator, employee or affiliate copy where the paid or gifted relationship is not visible up front.

Honest opinion and no rewording (16 CFR 255.1(a), (b)):

> "Endorsements must reflect the honest opinions, findings, beliefs, or experience of the endorser. Furthermore, an endorsement may not convey any express or implied representation that would be deceptive if made directly by the advertiser."

> "However, the endorsement may not be presented out of context or reworded so as to distort in any way the endorser's opinion or experience with the product."

What to flag in a headline: a quote in quotation marks that is not the endorser's exact words from the raw material, or a trimmed quote that changes its meaning.

Typical results (16 CFR 255.2(b)):

> "An advertisement containing an endorsement relating the experience of one or more consumers on a central or key attribute of the product will likely be interpreted as representing that the endorser's experience is representative of what consumers will generally achieve with the advertised product in actual, albeit variable, conditions of use. Therefore, an advertiser should possess and rely upon adequate substantiation for this representation. If the advertiser does not have substantiation that the endorser's experience is representative of what consumers will generally achieve, the advertisement should clearly and conspicuously disclose the generally expected performance in the depicted circumstances, and the advertiser must possess and rely on adequate substantiation for that representation."

What to flag in a headline: "I lost 20 lbs", "She paid off $30k" style results from one customer presented as what buyers will get.

Actual consumers (16 CFR 255.2(c)):

> "Advertisements presenting endorsements by what are represented, expressly or by implication, to be “actual consumers” should utilize actual consumers in both the audio and video, or clearly and conspicuously disclose that the persons in such advertisements are not actual consumers of the advertised product."

What to flag in a headline: a fictional persona or scene written in first person so that it reads as a real customer (links to the "Fictional label" check in section 0).

Agencies are liable too (16 CFR 255.1(f)):

> "Advertising agencies, public relations firms, review brokers, reputation management companies, and other similar intermediaries may be liable for their roles in creating or disseminating endorsements containing representations that they know or should know are deceptive."

What to flag in a headline: nothing separate; this is why the client step flags instead of passing risky endorsement copy along.

Source for all of 2.1: https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-16/chapter-I/subchapter-B/part-255 (text fetched from the eCFR API, checked 2026-10-02)

### 2.2 Trade Regulation Rule on the Use of Consumer Reviews and Testimonials, 16 CFR Part 465 (89 FR 68077, Aug. 22, 2024)

Fake reviews and testimonials (16 CFR 465.2(a)):

> "It is an unfair or deceptive act or practice and a violation of this part for a business to write, create, or sell a consumer review, consumer testimonial, or celebrity testimonial that materially misrepresents, expressly or by implication:
> (1) That the reviewer or testimonialist exists;
> (2) That the reviewer or testimonialist used or otherwise had experience with the product, service, or business that is the subject of the review or testimonial; or
> (3) The reviewer's or testimonialist's experience with the product, service, or business that is the subject of the review or testimonial."

What to flag in a headline: any review, star rating, testimonial or "customers say" line that is not taken word for word from the raw material. Invented reviews are prohibited, and this includes reviews generated by this pipeline or any AI tool. Note: the rule text covers anything a business "write[s], create[s], or sell[s]" and does not name AI; the FTC's own statement that the rule reaches AI-generated reviews is `[unverified]` here (check the FTC's August 2024 announcement or the Federal Register preamble at 89 FR 68077).

Buying reviews for a sentiment (16 CFR 465.4):

> "It is an unfair or deceptive act or practice and a violation of this part for a business to provide compensation or other incentives in exchange for, or conditioned expressly or by implication on, the writing or creation of consumer reviews expressing a particular sentiment, whether positive or negative, regarding the product, service, or business that is the subject of the review."

What to flag in a headline: "Leave a 5-star review and get..." offers.

Review suppression (16 CFR 465.7(b)):

> "For a business to materially misrepresent, expressly or by implication, that the consumer reviews of one or more of the products or services it sells displayed in a portion of its website or platform dedicated in whole or in part to receiving and displaying consumer reviews represent most or all the reviews submitted to the website or platform when reviews are being suppressed (i.e., not displayable) based upon their ratings or their negative sentiment."

What to flag in a headline: "4.9 stars from all our reviews", "every customer loves it" when the raw material does not show the full review set.

Fake social influence (16 CFR 465.8(b)):

> "Purchase or procure fake indicators of social media influence that they knew or should have known to be fake and that materially misrepresent their influence or importance for a commercial purpose."

What to flag in a headline: follower, download or "1M users" counts that are not in the raw material with a source.

Insider reviews (16 CFR 465.5(a)):

> "It is an unfair or deceptive act or practice and a violation of this part for an officer or manager of a business to write or create a consumer review or consumer testimonial about the business or one of the products or services it sells that fails to have a clear and conspicuous disclosure of the officer's or manager's material relationship to the business, unless, in the case of a consumer testimonial, the relationship is otherwise clear to the audience."

What to flag in a headline: founder or staff testimonials framed as ordinary customer voices.

Clear and conspicuous on social media (16 CFR 465.1(c)(4)):

> "In any communication using an interactive electronic medium, such as social media or the internet, the disclosure must be unavoidable. A disclosure is not clear and conspicuous if a consumer must take any action, such as clicking on a hyperlink or hovering over an icon, to see it."

What to flag in a headline: a required disclosure placed behind "See more", in a link, or only in a later frame.

Source for all of 2.2: https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-16/chapter-I/subchapter-B/part-465 (text fetched from the eCFR API, checked 2026-10-02)

## 3. Industry-specific (when the industry is given)

### 3.1 Health and wellness claims for consumer products that are not medical devices

FDA device definition, 21 U.S.C. 321(h)(1) (FD&C Act section 201(h)):

> "The term “device” (except when used in paragraph (n) of this section and in sections 331(i), 343(f), 352(c), and 362(c) of this title) means an instrument, apparatus, implement, machine, contrivance, implant, in vitro reagent, or other similar or related article, including any component, part, or accessory, which is— [...] (B) intended for use in the diagnosis of disease or other conditions, or in the cure, mitigation, treatment, or prevention of disease, in man or other animals, or (C) intended to affect the structure or any function of the body of man or other animals, and which does not achieve its primary intended purposes through chemical action within or on the body of man or other animals and which is not dependent upon being metabolized for the achievement of its primary intended purposes."

Source: https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/21/321 (checked 2026-10-02; extra spaces added by the site's link markup removed)

What to flag in a headline: words that make the product "intended for" diagnosis, cure, mitigation, treatment or prevention of a disease ("treats", "cures", "prevents", "diagnoses", "relieves [disease]"). Intended use is shown by the claims, so the headline itself can move a product into device territory.

FDA guidance "General Wellness: Policy for Low Risk Devices" (issued January 6, 2026, supersedes the September 27, 2019 version; nonbinding):

> "For purposes of this guidance, CDRH defines general wellness products as products that meet the following two factors: (1) are intended for only general wellness use, as defined in this guidance, and (2) present a low risk to the safety of users and other persons."

> "The first category of general wellness intended uses involve claims about sustaining or offering general improvement to functions associated with a general state of health that do not make any reference to diseases or conditions."

The guidance lists that first category as weight management, physical fitness, relaxation or stress management, mental acuity, self-esteem, sleep management, and sexual function. Its examples of claims outside the category:

> "The following are examples of claims that do not fall into this category of general wellness claims: • A claim that a product will treat or diagnose obesity; • A claim that a product will treat an eating disorder, such as anorexia; • A claim that a product helps treat an anxiety disorder; • A claim that a computer game will diagnose or treat autism; • A claim that a product will treat muscle atrophy or erectile dysfunction; and • A claim to restore a structure or function impaired due to a disease or condition, e.g., a claim that a prosthetic device enables amputees to walk."

> "Products are not general wellness products if their labeling, advertising, user interface, or functionality includes any of the following: 1. references to specific diseases, clinical conditions, or diagnostic thresholds; 2. alerts, alarms, or prompts that recommend or require specific clinical action or medical management; 3. treatment guidance intended to inform or direct medical management; 4. claims of clinical equivalence, clinical accuracy, medical or clinical grade, or substitution for an FDA-authorized, cleared, or approved medical device; or 5. intended-use statements that explicitly target diagnosis, screening, monitoring, or management of a disease or condition."

Disease-referencing wellness claims are allowed only in the guidance's second category, and only in the "as part of a healthy lifestyle, may help reduce the risk of / may help living well with" form for chronic diseases where that link is generally accepted.

Source: https://www.fda.gov/media/90652/download (PDF; landing page https://www.fda.gov/regulatory-information/search-fda-guidance-documents/general-wellness-policy-low-risk-devices returned "Not found" to curl on 2026-10-02) (checked 2026-10-02; bullet characters rendered as "•")

What to flag in a headline: disease or condition names (insomnia, anxiety disorder, ADHD, hypertension, diabetes, obesity), "medical-grade", "clinically accurate", "as accurate as a [medical device]", "replaces your [device]", or any alert/treatment promise; safe ground is wellness language with no disease reference ("sleep better", "manage stress").

### 3.2 Food and dietary supplements (brief)

Structure/function claims for dietary supplements, 21 U.S.C. 343(r)(6) (DSHEA):

> "(C) the statement contains, prominently displayed and in boldface type, the following: “This statement has not been evaluated by the Food and Drug Administration. This product is not intended to diagnose, treat, cure, or prevent any disease.”. A statement under this subparagraph may not claim to diagnose, mitigate, treat, cure, or prevent a specific disease or class of diseases."

Source: https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/21/343 (checked 2026-10-02; extra spaces from link markup removed). The disclaimer rule is written for labeling; how it applies to an ad headline is `[unverified]` here.

What to flag in a headline: supplement or food copy that names a disease or promises to diagnose, mitigate, treat, cure or prevent one ("supports immunity against flu", "lowers blood sugar"); structure/function wording ("supports healthy sleep") needs substantiation in the raw material and, on labeling, the disclaimer above.

### 3.3 "Made in USA", 16 CFR Part 323 (brief)

> "In connection with promoting or offering for sale any good or service, in or affecting commerce as “commerce” is defined in section 4 of the Federal Trade Commission Act, 15 U.S.C. 44, it is an unfair or deceptive act or practice within the meaning of section 5(a)(1) of the Federal Trade Commission Act, 15 U.S.C. 45(a)(1), to label any product as Made in the United States unless the final assembly or processing of the product occurs in the United States, all significant processing that goes into the product occurs in the United States, and all or virtually all ingredients or components of the product are made and sourced in the United States."

Source: https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-16/chapter-I/subchapter-B/part-323 (text fetched from the eCFR API, checked 2026-10-02). Part 323 itself covers labels and mail-order catalog labels; "Made in USA" in other ads falls under Section 5 and the FTC's enforcement policy statement, which is `[unverified]` here.

What to flag in a headline: "Made in USA", "American-made", "Built in America", "Crafted in [US state]" with no origin facts in the raw material.
